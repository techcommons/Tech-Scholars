Sarah Emma Edmonds

Sarah Emma Edmonds was born in Canada in 1841. Her father did not treat her well so she ran away from home to the US disguising herself as a man named Franklin Thompson. Franklin Thompson was quite good at their job of selling bibles but when the war broke out felt they had to help the union cause and the best way to do that was to join the army. Thompson did many odd jobs for the army including being a mail carrier, a hospital attendant, and ferrying wounded soldiers. Thompson was hurt very badly fighting in the Battle of Second Manassas and lived the rest of their life with pain in their legs. Thompson in 1863 contracted Malaria. Afraid of being discovered, they left and Franklin Thompson was declared a deserter. After service Edmonds married Linus Seelye and they had 3 children together. In 1876 she attended a reunion of Civil War veterans and was greeted kindly even though, at this point, she was presenting as a woman. Her fellow soldiers even helped her clear the charge of desertion, which made her eligible for a military pension and helped provide for her family, despite the injuries she had obtained in the war stopping a bit of her mobility. She wrote a best selling novel "Nurse and Spy in the Union Army" detailing her experience in the war. Edmonds was admitted to the Grand Army of the Republic as their only ever female member and was buried with military honors upon her death in 1901. Edmonds was a fiercely patriotic woman who in an action mostly associated with men felt that the best way to serve her country was not privately at home but on the front lines fighting. Edmonds' choices to keep herself in the public eye through the publishing of her memoir, attending of military reunions, and admission into a prestigious military fraternity shows Edmonds refusal to be relegated to a private field.

Loreta Janeta Velázquez

Loreta Janeta Velázquez was born in Cuba in 1842. Her family moved around a lot around the North American continent. She dreamed of a life outside her high class station as a woman with lots of adventure and attention. At 14 Velazquez eloped with an American soldier. Velazquez had 3 children all of whom died in infancy which broke her heart but also affirmed her ambition. At the start of the Civil War Velazquez's husband William defected from the US army and joined the confederates. Velazquez wanted to join the army from the start but her husband said she couldn't go so after he left on his service she enlisted as Henry T. Buford without his knowledge. Buford revealed themself to William after recruiting over 200 men to the confederate cause. William died in battle and Buford continued to fight now as a widow. Buford spied for the confederates in Washington D. C. Dressed up now as a woman as Velazquez knew no one would suspect her of being the spy she was. She gained military secrets from her husband's old army friends and sent them back to the confederate army. After the war she got married 2 more times and had a son. In 1876 she published a book "The Woman in Battle: A Narrative of the Exploits, Adventures and Travels of Madame Loreta Janeta Velazquez, Otherwise Known as Lieutenant Harry J. Buford, Confederate States Army". It is unclear how much of the book is fictionalized or embellished to make a more compelling story. It is also unclear when she died. What is known is that Velazquez was ambitious and wanted adventure and glory like most soldiers. Velazquez used her femininity to her advantage to advance her mission and her own personal goals. Velazquez used gender expression to her advantage using her identity as Harry J. Buford to expand her military recognition and adventure and her identity as Loreta Janeta Velázquez to expand her personal goals for glory.

Albert Cashier

Albert Cashier was assigned female at birth and given the name Jennie Hodgers. Born in Ireland not much is known about Cashier's early life or how they made their way to the US. In 1862 Cahier enlisted in the 95th Illinois Infantry. Cashier fought in over 40 battles and was considered by all of their regiment to be a good soldier. Cashier served for the full 3 year enlistment and then returned to Illinois. What makes Cashier so interesting and unique is that unlike a majority of the people who were assigned female at birth who fought in the war, Cashier continued living as a man. This has led historians to believe that Cashier was a transgender man. Cashier's lived their life like any other veteran working odd jobs around their town, collecting their veteran pensions and voting in elections despite the fact that women still did not have the right to vote. In an event very similar to a lot of gender non conforming soldiers their sex was discovered after an injury. In 1910 after living 45 years as a man Cashier was hit by a car and had to go to the hospital. Cashier was sent to the Soldiers and Sailors Home in Quincy, Illinois who agreed to keep Cashier's sex hidden but 3 years later after Cashier developed dementia they were sent to an insane asylum. Due to their dementia Cashier could not fight back when the hospital workers put them in women's clothes and publicly published a story about Albert Cashier as Jennie Hodgers. Many soldiers who fought with Cashier actually came to their defence and denounced how the hospital was treating Cashier. In 1915 Cashier died and was given the full respect of a soldier being buried in their uniform and having their military service and the name Albert Cashier being displayed on their tomb. Cashier tried to live a more private life, showing their gender expression privately. Cashier did not write a book about their experience in the army like the other two people I talked about. They tried to live a normal life as a man and did not want to be recognized.

The past is not known for being kind to queer people. People assume that queer people had to be completely hidden or only express themselves in private to survive. However that might not be entirely true when looking at the gender non conforming soldiers of the Civil War. When soldiers were asked if they knew of any women fighting in the war most of them said yes and that they had knowingly fought with people who they knew were not men. There were even terms for gender non conforming people during the time of the civil war; they were called female-husbands, showing that people knew gender non conforming people existed. So if most of these gender non conforming people never faced any consequences for being who they are, how taboo was being queer actually?
