The Art of Feminism

Feminism

is defined as the "belief in and advocacy of the political, economic, and social equality of the sexes."

The Second Wave

The purpose of this website is to showcase artists and artworks relevant to the Feminist Art Movement. To better understand the Feminist Art Movement, it is important to consider the historical context in which it emerged. The movement began during the second wave of feminism, alongside various other civil and cultural movements in the late 1960s and early 1970s. This period marked a transformative era of social change in American History. With the growing momentum of the Civil Rights Movement, demands for racial equality, women's rights, gay liberation, environmentalism, and anti-war activism were gaining national traction. Women were done waiting their turn. Betty Friedan's 1963 book The Feminine Mystique had already cracked open the myth of the happy housewife, expressing discontent millions of women across the country felt. Friedan's book ignited the second feminist wave, encouraging women to fight gender oppression. By 1966, that frustration became an organization. The National Organization for Women (NOW) demanded equal pay, reproductive rights, and an end to workplace discrimination.

However, second wave feminism was not a perfect, all-encompassing feminist movement; it had its own blind spots. Black feminists formed the National Black Feminist Organization (NBFO) in 1973 to provide an alternative to mainstream feminist movements that too often centered the experiences of white, middle-class women while ignoring the compounded weight of race and class. Lesbian feminists also demanded visibility within a movement that sometimes sidelined their participation. The resulting movement was not a single, tidy feminism but a chorus of overlapping, sometimes contradictory voices, all insisting that women's lives, in their full complexity, mattered. Following the 1963 Equal Pay Act, more legal victories drove this wave of Feminism foreward. By 1972, Title IX had opened the doors of education and athletics; by 1973, Roe v. Wade had affirmed a woman's right to her own body. The passage of these laws was no doubt a victory, but laws can not, on their own, dismantle the deeper exclusion of women in museums, classrooms, history books, and other aspects of society. That work fell to the artists.

The Feminist Art Movement

The Feminist Art Movement worked to challenge male dominance in both art and society, pushing for recognition and equality for female artists while questioning long-held assumptions about womanhood. Before this movement, most female artists worked largely in personal, private spheres, outside of public view, often denied exhibitions and gallery representation simply based on their sex. The art world was largely known as a boys' club, where the hard-drinking, womanizing members of Abstract Expressionism were glamorized. In response, feminist artists turned to materials and mediums that had been overlooked or dismissed by the male-dominated art world. This often meant embracing materials like textiles and other forms of craft long associated with "women's work" to create. Performance and video, mediums without the same centuries of male-dominated tradition that painting and sculpture carried, offered further room for experimentation. The Feminist Art Movement created opportunities and spaces for women and minority artists that had not previously existed while simultaneously questioning why these barriers to access were in place.
