Weinberger v. Wiesenfeld and the Strikedown of Gendered Law

By Aarna Kapur

Ruth Bader Ginsburg (1933-2020) was a women's rights activist and a Supreme Court Justice who permanently transformed gender equality under the law. She cofounded the ACLU Women's Rights Project in 1972, and under Ginsburg's guidance, the ACLU successfully argued cases before the Supreme Court to make gender discrimination unconstitutional. Each case is not always glamorous, but each case effectively paved the road to make the law more equitable one case at a time.

One of these cases was called the Weinberger v. Wiesenfeld case (01/20/1975 - 03/19/1975), where Ginsburg presented an oral argument, where she not only fought for her clients' rights, but taught an all-male court how gender stereotypes against women consequently affected men under the law. The basis of this case was a father who applied for Social Security survivor benefits after his wife died. His wife was the primary income source of the household, and at the time he was denied because the law assumed that only mothers needed support. Ginsburg took this case to illustrate the harm that gender stereotyped laws caused. Ginsburg further used this case to prove that, according to the law, a woman's contributions were valued less than a man's. The law assumed women were dependents; once she struck that down, it led to a pivotal win of equal pay for women.

Ginsburg opened her oral argument explaining the issue by telling the court that the case was a prime example of gender based discrimination. Telling them it is a relic of an era when it is normalized to treat men and women in two distinct different classes and roles in society under the law. When it was time for the court to ask questions and the male Justices question if the law was intended to help widows support their children after their husband passed. Ginsburg turned the argument around and explained how intent often masks double standards that lock men out of caregiving roles and diminish women's economic contributions. She further forcefully argued that women's salaries are not pin money or a secondary income, and by denying widower benefits, the government was falsely claiming that women's financial contributions were secondary and her household was worth less than a man's. Her argument was logical and airtight; she won the case unanimously (8-0) and struck down the gender based distinction in Social Security and survivor benefits.
