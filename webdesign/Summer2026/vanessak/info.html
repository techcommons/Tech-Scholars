The Great Depression

The Great Depression (1929-1939) was one of the most difficult economic challenges and disasters in American history. It left about a quarter of the U.S Workforce unemployed and as a result, many men lost their jobs, and the ability to provide for their families. So, women played more crucial roles than before. Some women even entered the workforce, mostly as nurses, secretaries, or teachers. But most women remained housewives, and their unpaid labor was just as essential of an occupation. These women would manage their household's budgets, find creative ways to make their limited resources into satiating meals, and work to provide healthy plates despite the shrinking incomes and rising costs of groceries. Historians argue that the country could not have survived the crisis without women's imperative contributions, since "no housewife lost her job in the Depression." Along with the economic strain, there were other hardships such as family breakdowns, higher rates of homelessness, divorce, and overall emotional stress leading to higher rates of suicide. Race also played a major role, as African Americans and other minorities faced greater unemployment, lower wages, and fewer opportunities than before and in comparison to white women amid the Great Depression. President Franklin D. Roosevelt, who served during the second half of the Depression, put into place the New Deal which sought to provide financial relief and employment programs, but it often favored men which meant that women had to rely on themselves while also organizing strikes and protesting for fairer treatment.Although in some cases, such as in the image below which is a Sewing Project, women were employed by New Deal Agencies to receive relief.

Budgeting and Feeding Families

While this crisis came with many struggles, one of the greatest challenges women faced was feeding their families with their limited budgets. Prices of foods such as eggs, milk, and fresh vegetables became extremely difficult to afford, so housewives had to find innovative ways to to prepare nutritious meals for their family. Community potlucks became a popular choice as they allow neighbors to share food, stretch their budgets, and provide social support during these hard times. At the same time, mass-produced food items such as margarine, mayonnaise, and canned soup became household staples because they were affordable for families and were extremely versatile when preparing meals. Many companies during this era experienced major declines in sales after the stock market crashed in 1929, grocery stores remained relatively stable and even saw a slight increase because families had to prioritize buying food. Food companies often adapted and started to promote their products through recipes and meal ideas that showed women how to create filling dishes with inexpensive ingredients through advertisements. (such as the one shown on the left or right). Companies that invested in advertisements tended to perform better and gain more customers and sales. These budgeting strategies and choices made by consumers demonstrate how women played a vital role in helping both their families and communities to survive the Great Depression.
