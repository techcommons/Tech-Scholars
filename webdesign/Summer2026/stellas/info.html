Davis's legal troubles began after Jonathan Jackson, a young activist trying to free three imprisoned Black men known as the Soledad Brothers, took hostages outside a California courthouse in August 1970. The situation ended in a shootout that cost the lives of four people, including Jackson and the courtroom judge. Because Davis was linked to the firearms involved in the incident, she was charged and was at risk of facing the death penalty.


Davis went underground and the FBI placed her on the "Ten Most Wanted" list, prompting President Nixon to laud her capture as the arrest of a "dangerous terrorist." Davis spent around eighteen months in jail, most of it in solitary confinement, before an all-white jury acquitted her of all charges on June 4, 1972.



Her imprisonment catalyzed a public movement that spanned legal to cultural spheres. The Angela Davis Legal Defense Committee organized protests outside prisons, supporters wore pins bearing the slogan "Free Angela Davis," and musicians including John Lennon, Yoko Ono, and the Rolling Stones released songs referencing her case.

During a 1972 speech in Los Angeles, Davis described the momentum of the movement: "The surge of a massive popular resistance… the government could not through me, terrorize people who would openly demonstrate their opposition to racism, to war, to poverty, to repression." The public attention the movement gained turned Davis's individual legal case into a national reckoning over the factors of race and gender in incarceration and political repression.


The "Free Angela Davis" movement was thus significant in exposing how Black women faced overlapping forms of vulnerability tied to race, gender, and political affiliation. While jailed at the New York Women's House of Detention, Davis detailed her realizations in her 1974 autobiography: "I not only pressed harder for my own release, but I was becoming increasingly persuaded that something had to be done about this maximum security arrangement camouflaged as a therapeutic cellblock. Regardless of why the women in 4b had been placed there, they were all being horribly damaged." After her release, Davis went on to advocate, through speaking tours and her own renowned academic writings, for incarcerated women.

She critically thought about the intertwined role of race and gender in the criminal justice system, observing that Black women were eight times more likely to go to prison than white women. In an academic essay titled "Race, Gender, and the Prison Industrial Complex: California and Beyond," Davis argued that gendered violence within prisons—medical neglect, sexual abuse, and loss of parental rights—remained largely invisible to the public. Davis went on to contribute to organizations such as Critical Resistance and continue to advocate on the behalf of incarcerated women of color. The "Free Angela Davis" movement was thus not solely about one woman, but broadly opened space for discussion regarding racial and gendered incarceration.
