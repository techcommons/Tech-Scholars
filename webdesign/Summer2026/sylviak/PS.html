Overview

Gertrude Käsebier, "Zitkála-Šá", 1898. Smithsonian Institution, July 23, 2026

"The heart is always stronger than the mind, even if the mind is the wiser of the two."

When Zitkála-Šá was eight years old, she went to boarding school at the White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute. When the school staff forcefully cut her hair, she realized they had no respect for her culture and beliefs. After coming home three years later, Zitkála-Šá felt she no longer fully belonged either on the Yankton Indian Reservation or in white society.

As an adult, she got a job as a music teacher at the Carlisle Indian School. Realizing she was helping a system that was trying to destroy her culture, she turned to writing to preserve her Native American traditions while exposing the injustices indigenous people faced. In her work "The School Days of an Indian Girl", she described the forced assimilation, the loss of her native language, and the pressure to abandon her culture. She was also a strong defender of Native American spiritual beliefs challenging well-established stereotypes. Her writing helped many people to better understand the harmful effects of federal assimilation policies on Native Americans.
