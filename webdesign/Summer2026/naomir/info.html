About American Cookery

"Adapted to this country, and all grades of life."

— American Cookery, Title Page, 1776
Author Unknown, The basement kitchen of seventeenth-century Bacon's Castle in Surry County, Virginia, with brick hearth and timber lintel. In the eighteenth century, an outside kitchen—a summer kitchen—was built, Summer 2007. Colonial Williamsburg, 7/24/2026

Published in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1776, American Cookery was a slim volume that combined British cooking traditions with local American ingredients. It was published "according to Act of Congress," showing that even early American authors were protecting their work through copyright.

Simmons included classic British recipes such as Queen's Cake and Plum Cake, reflecting the continued influence of British cuisine. At the same time, she introduced recipes using ingredients common in America, including Johnny Cake, Indian Slapjack, Buckwheat Cake, Cranberry Sauce, and Pumpkin Pie. This blend of traditions marked the beginning of a unique American cuisine.

American Cookery was also the first cookbook to use pearlash, an early American chemical leavener that was the predecessor of modern baking soda. The cookbook contains instructions for preparing meats, fish, poultry, vegetables, and a variety of cakes ranging from elegant desserts to simple everyday recipes.

By using ingredients that were available throughout the new nation, Simmons created recipes that were practical for people from all walks of life, from modest households to wealthier families.

The cookbook also reflected the gender roles of 1776, a time when women were expected to manage the home and prepare meals for their families. By writing her cookbook for "young females," Simmons provided practical knowledge that helped women succeed in their domestic responsibilities. At the same time, her success as the first American woman to publish a cookbook showed that women could influence the nation's culture and identity through their knowledge and writing.
