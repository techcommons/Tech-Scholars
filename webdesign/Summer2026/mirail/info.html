Introduction

In 1651, the widow Jeane Gardiner of Hamilton Tribe was accused of bewitching her neighbor Tomasin, a case that would later escalate to her execution later that month. This was the first event of five "witch" executions occurring between 1651 and 1655 in Bermuda.

What prompted the witch accusations?

Matthew Hopkins' Discovery of Witches. 1648. Public Domain Image Archive. Accessed July 24, 2026. https://pdimagearchive.org/images/83e2087e-15a3-4259-8195-18bccb89a651/.

During England's own witch hunting craze, 200,000 alleged witches were executed within 266 years. With the large death toll came the popularization of witch hunters such as Matthew Hopkins, the famous "witchfinder general," whose ideas likely spread to Bermuda causing hysteria over witches in the first place. Aside from these preexisting tensions within Bermuda, there were also stark similarities which could be drawn between the victims of the accusations. Out of the five casualties, four of which being women, the majority were spinsters, meaning women who spun fibers into yarn as an occupation. Additionally, every single convicted witch was identifiable as a society reject. This ensured that the accused would lack the resources or support to escape their role as a societal scapegoat. This is exemplified by Anne Bowen, a woman who had been accused at the same time as Jeane Gardiner and yet was ruled innocent due to her familial relevance.

Case chronology

The "sink or float" method of seeking out witches. Woodcut circa 1720. Public Domain Image Archive. https://pdimagearchive.org/images/1fc74653-d7d1-4320-8df2-38508d92cd43/.

Jeane Gardiner's accusations stemmed from her alleged torture of her neighbor Tomasin, a mixed woman, although the relationship between the two was mostly undefined. Tomasin claimed to have undergone extreme pain and a brief loss of vision following the incident. The accusations spiraled from this point with other community members chiming in that they too had been targeted by Gardiner, resulting in a 12 woman committee to inspect her. Hopkins had decreed that a witch could be identified by "a little teate" and "blewish spots" which likely referred to what are now known as skin tags and moles. Additionally, Gardiner was thrown in the ocean twice, yet floated both times, another "telltale" sign of being a witch, despite her efforts to sink. Although she pleaded not guilty for the entire case, she was still executed on May 26th. Gardiner's execution was the first in 11 years on Gallow's Island (now known as Ordnance Island) and therefore drew a large viewing.
