Zitkala-Sa

Early suffragists turned to indigenous communities as examples of what women's suffrage could look like. Despite this, in 1920, when the 19th amendment was passed, indigenous women could not be citizens and therefore could not vote. Indigenous advocates like Zitkala-Ša from the Yankton Dakota Sioux Tribe continued to advocate for suffrage, however, and in 1924, the Snyder Act allowed indigenous women to be citizens. Yet, it was still nearly 40 years before barriers like literacy tests or poll taxes were removed.

1911 Referendum in California, Reaching Spanish Speaking Voters

In 1911, a referendum was added to the California ballot to allow women to vote in local and state level elections. Many women advocated for the passing of this referendum, which had previously failed. Despite only 5% of the California population being Spanish speaking, key suffragists like María Guadalupe Evangelina de Lopéz gave speeches and translated fliers into Spanish as they understood that every vote mattered (see below). The referendum passed narrowly by 3,500 votes out of about 150,000 people. This document shows how effective advocacy often involves language accessibility and educating and mobilizing all people, not just those with the most concentrated power or privilege.
