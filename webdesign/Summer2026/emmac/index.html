Not only is women's history often excluded from textbooks and dominant narratives, the version of women's history that is told is slim. While only 13% of history in US History textbooks represents women, 0% of that is typically AAPI women. Specifically, when it comes to the women's suffrage movement, oftentimes, white upper-middle class women are spotlighted. And, it's important to recognize that the 19th amendment (1920) did not bring the right to vote for many women of color, who spent years continuing to fight for this right. Hence, this exhibit aims to diversify historical perspectives on the women's suffrage movement through primary sources and histories of women of color.

Figures like Mabel Lee (1897-1966) challenge these dominant narratives. Mabel Lee was a Chinese American who was able to immigrate to the United States at a young age since her father was a Baptist missionary. Despite not being able to vote herself as the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act barred her from becoming a citizen, Mabel Lee was influential throughout the Progressive Era and Women's Suffrage movement in advocating for women's right to vote.

Why This Matters

Oftentimes, AAPIs are seen as a model minority and as if they are passive participants in society. While the model minority is a complex topic, it was coined from William Peterson's 1966 New York Times article titled, "Success Story, Japanese-American Style", in which he praised Japanese Americans for their success despite facing much discrimination. However, this idea of the model minority also suggests other minorities are "problem minorities" for demanding resources or struggle due to systemic oppression. Instead, the model minority myth suggests that all minority people should be able to succeed on their own merit and be passive and successful participants in society.

Some believe that women's history or AAPI history should be included because they did something "good" or because AAPIs are the "model minority". But, since history and humanities attempts to explain past human actions and interactions, it's important to remember that this history shouldn't just be included due to Lee's accomplishments or "goodness", but because AAPI women are people too, who have influenced and lived through history.

Not only does Lee serve as a powerful example of intersectionality and how layers of oppression intersect, but she also serves as a historical reminder that AAPI people, AAPI women, have and will continue to be politically active, powerful, and present through history.
