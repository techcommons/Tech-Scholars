Riot Grrrl Origins

Riot grrrl isn't the only sub genre of punk, but it is the one most connected to feminist activism.The punk movement itself developed around the 70s, but Riot grrrl didn't come to be until the 90s. Essentially, riot grrrl differs from traditional punk because they have a large focus on defying traditional women's roles rather than a more general rebellion against societal norms. Prior to this movement women were aiming to break away from domestic roles with equal access to the same rights men exercised. Grrrls wanted to be recognized as individuals with economic independence, bodily autonomy, and equal rights without discrimination based on gender. In a lot of media they can be recognized by their complete opposition to feminine standards using vulgar language or ways of dressing. Though they challenge conventional expectations grrrls also reclaim symbols of femininity, accessories, dresses, makeup turning them into elements of empowerment rather than conformity.


Grrrls Through the Decades

Patti Smith - 70s

Smith is an artist, musician, and writer. Punk is commonly associated with rock, so when Smith incorporated poetry into her music she redefined what punk could be. She contributed greatly to the rise of punk poets. Smith was also known for her androgynous look, which raised questions about gender identity and what women could be.


Frightwig - 80s

Frightwig is a band that completely rejects traditional feminine stereotypes. They use obscene language, cacophonous music, and other provocative actions during their performances, taking hardcore music, which is usually seen as masculine, and presenting it in a new way. Their music helped fuel the rise of riot grrrl and inspired another widely known band, Bikini Kill.


Bikini Kill - 90s

Bikini Kill is one of the most well-known bands from the riot grrrl movement. They wanted to unapologetically set their own standards and define women's bodily autonomy. They spoke out against gender inequality and sexual violence. Members of Bikini Kill encouraged women to make space for themselves with their music and zines, including the Riot Grrrl Manifesto.
