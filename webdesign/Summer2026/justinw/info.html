The Impossible Made Beautiful: Martha Ann Honeywell

Subject: Martha Ann Honeywell (1786–1856) Collection: Early American Performers & Artists

01 / Biographical Overview

Martha Ann Honeywell was an American artist and performer who created silhouettes—a type of profile portrait—using only her mouth and toes. Honeywell grew up without forearms and hands, and with only three toes on one foot. One of her advertisements claimed she stood three feet tall. Growing up in Westchester, New York, her career gained early traction after moving to New York City's Lower East Side with her family, which included five siblings. At times, she was put on display at her father's fruit store.

As early as age 12, Honeywell had her first public appearance at New York's American Museum, where she was seen sewing and flipping through book pages using merely three toes. From there, her career took off, leading to a tour that reached 32 American cities and extended across the Atlantic to Europe.

02 / Critical Analysis & Cultural Context

Why Honeywell?

Honeywell charged 50¢ for entry, and another 25¢ if one wanted their silhouette cut which would equate to either 13 or 6.5 dollars. And the late 18th and early 19th centuries, before photography emerged, consumers frequently chose silhouettes as an affordable alternative to elaborate painted portraits, but the art also held a romantic appeal with these silhouettes being love tokens from deployed soldiers, or even deceased family members. Often referred to simply as "shades," these dark, striking profile cuts soared in popularity during the late 18th and early 19th centuries alongside a growing cultural obsession with Neoclassicism. The clean, stark lines of a shadow profile mirrored the timeless elegance of ancient Greek vase paintings and Roman relief coins, giving ordinary people a way to participate in high-style Regency aesthetics without paying aristocratic prices.

While the 25¢ silhouettes Honeywell charged for held popular appeal, her work mainly revolved around putting her disability on display. In the 19th century, circulating ideas about disability often framed physical differences as subjects of intense curiosity or religious wonder. Honeywell strategically used marketing tactics that played into these Western cultural beliefs to draw attention. Beyond her physical condition, she was known for a personality that presented acceptable class and gender traits, while also being viewed as uniquely American.

On one silhouette, Honeywell inscribed, "Cut without hands by M. A. Honeywell." Beyond providing historical proof of her technique, the piece reveals a self-assured artist independently directing her public image. It took far more than a hesitant performer to stand on stage before queens and presidents.
