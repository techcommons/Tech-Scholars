The International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union: Fabric and Fire

With the advent of new inventions during the American industrial expansion of the early 1900s, the garment industry grew exponentially. In New York specifically, one third of the adult work force worked in the garment industry. The majority of garment workers were unmarried immigrant women who were forced to work in harsh conditions with little pay. Women worked for twelve to fourteen hours, six to seven days a week, maybe earning two dollars a day with only one bathroom break. In addition, workers would have to bring their own supplies like sewing machines. Mistakes or latenesses were punished with fines or dismissal. The factories themselves were extremely unsanitary and unsafe, sometimes posing extreme fire hazards.

The International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union (ILGWU) was founded to address these problems. The union served as a means for working immigrant women to make their voices heard, and would come to define early twentieth century labor relations through famous strikes such as "The Uprising of 20,000."

Social Unionism

Institution:Cornell University ILR School

The ILGWU also developed social programs in arts and education, health car, housing, and recreation. The ILGWU partnered with high schools and colleges to offer classes and training to members and to provide scholarships for higher education. The ILGWU also organized theater and music productions. In celebration of the union's fiftieth anniversary, the union produced and screened a movie that was later distributed nationally. Finally, the ILGWU provided affordable housing programs, even constructing resorts as inexpensive vacation homes for members.

Inequality in the ILGWU

Institution:Cornell University ILR School

Leaders of the ILGWU often didn't reflect the demographics of most members. While the union itself consisted of over 85% women of all races and ethnicities, every president of the ILGWU was a white man. Union vice president Rose Pessota was extremely critical of this disparity in leadership. Famously, she said "the voice of a solitary woman on the General Executive Board would be a voice lost in the wilderness."
