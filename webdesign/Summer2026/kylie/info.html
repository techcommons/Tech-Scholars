Emily Warren Roebling: The Secret Pilar behind the Brooklyn Bridge

Emily W. Roebling was born September 23rd, in 1843. She was born in Cold Spring, a village located in the town of Philipstown of Putnam County, New York. She was born to an influential and affluent family. Her father, Sylvanus Warren, was a politician that served in the New York State Assembly. Emily was the second youngest of twelve children. Due to her family's financial standing, Emily attended an esteemed all-girls school; the Georgetown Visitation Convent. While Emily had many siblings, one of her most notable was, Governeur K. Warren. In 1863, Warren served as Chief Topographical Engineer of the Army of Potomac during the Battle of Gettysburg, and earned the title of "The Hero of Little Round Top". In 1864, Emily had been invited by her brother to visit him at his camp. During this visit, she met her soon to be husband, Colonel Washington Roebling. In 1865, they were married. Despite their marriage, Colonel Roebling still had to report to his duties and continued to serve in the Union Army along with Gouverneur K. Warren. John Augustus Roebling, Emily's father-in-law, was a world-renowned architect that specialized in suspension bridges. In 1867, he received a project to build a 1.1-mile bridge. This bridge would span across the East River from Brooklyn to Manhattan and had never been built before. In the same year, Emily became pregnant and the young couple took a honeymoon to Europe. However, this simple vacation began the chapter that would transform Emily's life forever.

Emily The Engineer

John Roebling asked the pair to begin research for the new project. They began visiting construction sites and spoke with engineers that allowed them to gain knowledge about modern bridge-building techniques. The couple learned about cassions. During their trip, Emily gave birth to their son, John Roebling II. After their trip, they returned back to the U.S and settled in Brooklyn, to be near the designated construction site. In 1869, an incident occurred and John Roebling contracted tetanus from the injury. A few months later, he died and Washington became the chief engineer in the completion of the Brooklyn Bridge. Continuing the progress of the project, Washington worked underwater alongside fellow workers in the cassions. However, working in these conditions resulted in decompression sickness which, at the time, was unknown. Washington caught the sickness and ended up nearly paralyzed. He was no longer able to work on the project, and Emily took charge and began managing the project. She visited the construction site daily, and attended board meetings. Emily began studying onsite technical issues, durability of materials, cable construction, stress analysis and catenary curve calculations. She communicated orders and questions from both her husband and workers. Her work did not stop at the site, as she also represented her husband at social functions, kept records and answered mail. She was a pivotal part of the construction and took on all these responsibilities while keeping secret about her husband's illness in order to save their public image. On May 24th of 1883, Emily's job was completed, as the Brooklyn Bridge had opened, marking it as the longest suspension bridge of its time. She was the first person to walk across the bridge and had even been acknowledged by New York congressman Abram Hewitt for her dedication to the project.

A Persistent Powerhouse

After the completion of the bridge, Emily spent her time raising her son, John, who had a heart condition. John later got into and began attending Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, located in Troy, New York. After attending college, John got married and had moved to Arizona. Emily and her husband moved to Trenton, New Jersey. Emily was a member of the Federation of Women's Clubs and assisted in the creation of materials for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. In 1896, she travelled back to Europe and was received by Queen Victoria at court. She also travelled to St. Petersburg and attended the coronation of Czar Nicholas II of Russia. After returning to the U.S, she travelled to various states under the Federation of Women's Clubs to give lectures about her experiences in Russia. Additionally, she served as both a nurse and construction foreman at Montauk, Long Island Camp, for house soldiers returning from the Spanish American war.Through her civil work, Emily developed an interest for women's equality. To further her knowledge on this interest, Emily took a Women's Law Course at NYU and obtained her certificate in 1899. Under the program, Emily partook and won an essay contest. Her essay was later read at her own graduation. In her essay, she advocated for women's suffrage and the inequality of coverture. After her studies, Emily also became a member of the Daughters of the Revolution and had even been nominated for president of the organization in 1901. She continued to advocate for women to study law, suffrage and social services for the poor. On February 28th of 1903, Emily W. Warren passed away after battling stomach cancer. Despite her death, her work, sacrifice and dedication to women history still lives on.
