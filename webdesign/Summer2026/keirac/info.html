Early Life

Malintzin was born in the Yucatán Peninsula to an enslaved mother and a Nahua nobleman of Coatzacoalcos. Her parents named her Malinalli, after the goddess of grass, and added "Tenepal," meaning "one who speaks with liveliness." Growing up on the fringes of the Aztec Empire, Malintzin was no stranger to the horrors and devastation of war. Malintzin was only eight or nine years old when the Aztecs invaded Coatzacoalcos, forcing her mother to sell her into slavery. Torn from her family, Malinzen was loaded onto a canoe with other captives and sold to Maya traders in Xicallanco, a busy coastal port. From Xicallanco, she was transported further down the coast to Potonchán, in present-day Tabasco. As an enslaved girl, Malintzin was likely tasked with grinding corn, making tortillas, fetching water, caring for young children, sowing and harvesting the cotton plants, and working on textiles.

Conquest

In 1519, Chontal Maya leaders presented twenty enslaved women, including Malintzin, as a peace offering to Hernán Cortés's. Fluent in both Yucatec and Nahuatl, the languages of the Maya and Aztecs, Malintzin was quickly recognized for her linguistic talent. Throughout the conquest, she served as Cortés's interpreter, advisor, and guide, mediating relations between the Indigenous leaders and Spanish. Malinzin's role in the conquest was pivotal: In fact, she is featured in every illustration of meetings between Cortés and indigenous nobility and is occasionally depicted leading negotiations alone. Malintzin gave birth to Cortés's firstborn son, Martín, in 1522. Two years later, Cortés arranged her marriage to one of his captains, Juan Jaramillo, which granted her the legal status and privilege of a free Spanish noblewoman. She lived out her remaining years as a wealthy, respected figure before dying during a smallpox outbreak in 1529.
